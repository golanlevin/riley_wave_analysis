# Bridget Riley's Waves: A Harmonic Analysis

Golan Levin, June 2026

![bridget_riley_cataract3.webp](images/bridget_riley_standing_before_cataract3.webp)

*This study conducts a Fourier analysis of the wave contours in three paintings by Bridget Riley —* **Study for Arrest Series** *(1965),* **Cataract 3** *(1967) and* **Gala** *(1974) — in order to estimate the amount and character of their harmonic content/distortion (i.e., their deviation from being true sine waves).*

### Key Findings 

* **Riley's waves are genuine sine curves, to within a few percent**. Fourier analysis of *Study for Arrest Series* (1965), *Cataract 3* (1967), and *Gala* (1974) shows total harmonic distortion of just 3.4%, 1.2%, and 1.4% respectively. In geometric terms, these contours are *extremely* close to mathematical sine waves.
* **The waves in *Cataract 3* and *Gala* were almost certainly traced from a common master curve.** Across hundreds of traced contours, the same tiny departures from a perfect sine recur with remarkable consistency. The waves are not merely sinusoidal; they are copies of the same slightly imperfect sinusoid, strongly suggesting the use of a master curve that was transferred or traced repeatedly.
* **The residual errors suggest two kinds of construction evidence.** *Cataract 3* and *Gala* share evidence of template-based execution within each painting, but their templates appear different. By contrast, *Study for Arrest Series* and *Cataract 3* share unexpectedly similar residual harmonic signatures, despite their different scales, suggesting that the two 1960s works may have been generated by a related drafting procedure. In short: *Cataract 3* resembles *Gala* in how it was reproduced, while it resembles *Study for Arrest Series* in how its wave may have been constructed.

---

[Bridget Riley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridget_Riley) emerged in the early 1960s as one of the leading figures of Op Art, a movement concerned with the systematic exploration of visual perception. Riley's paintings use carefully organized geometric forms to generate sensations of movement, vibration, and visual instability. Her wave paintings represent one of the clearest expressions of this project. Although these works are often discussed in perceptual or phenomenological terms, relatively little attention has been paid to the exact geometry of the curves themselves. Are they merely wave-like, or are they mathematically sinusoidal? The answer turns out to be surprisingly precise.

Fourier analysis of the contours in Riley's wave paintings indicates that they are extraordinarily close approximations to sinusoidal curves rather than merely decorative undulations. Because Riley's earliest examples predate affordable scientific calculators (for example, the [HP-35](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HP-35) of 1972), and because there is no known evidence that she employed computer-assisted construction, as far as I am aware, one historically plausible means of generating such curves would have been manual geometric drafting. 

Today it is easy to forget that, before affordable electronic scientific calculators, entire books were devoted to numerical values of trigonometric functions. Reference works such as *Chambers's Mathematical Tables*, *Barlow's Tables*, and the *CRC Standard Mathematical Tables* contained page after page of precomputed sine and cosine values that could be used to construct accurate mathematical curves point-by-point on graph paper. While there is currently no direct evidence that Riley consulted any particular table book, her highly accurate sinusoidal forms are entirely compatible with this standard pre-digital drafting practice; their use by Riley remains an unproven but reasonable hypothesis.

## Method

Analysis script: [`wave_analysis.py`](wave_analysis.py)
(run with `.venv/bin/python wave_analysis.py`; needs numpy, opencv, scipy,
matplotlib).

1. Otsu-binarize each image (cleaned-up versions `riley_arrest.jpg`, `riley_gala.jpg` and `riley_cataract3.jpg`). For the black-and-white works this separates ink from paper; for the colored *Cataract 3* the same grayscale threshold separates pigment of any color from the near-white ground.
2. Find each pigment stripe as a connected component and trace its top and bottom boundaries as y(x) curves, one sample per pixel column. Each boundary is one copy of the wave: **14 usable copies** in *Study for Arrest Series*, **169** in *Cataract 3*, **156** in *Gala*.
3. Per copy: fit the wavelength by least squares (FFT initial guess), then fit a 12-harmonic Fourier series plus a linear drift term.
4. Phase offsets between copies are ignored by reporting translation-invariant quantities: amplitude ratios c<sub>n</sub>/c<sub>1</sub> and phases aligned so the fundamental sits at phase zero (ψ<sub>n</sub> = φ<sub>n</sub> − n·φ<sub>1</sub>).
5. The *phase coherence* of ψ<sub>n</sub> across copies (1.0 = identical in every copy, 0 = random) separates systematic shape features from brush/scan noise.

Canonical Fourier series, per painting:

> y(t) = Σ<sub>n</sub> c<sub>n</sub> · cos(n·t − ψ<sub>n</sub>),  where t = 2πx/λ


### Caveats

- Measurements are in JPEG pixel space of photographed artworks; sampling resolution, lens distortion, canvas texture, and compression set the noise floor.
- *Gala*'s copies span only 2.4 periods each, which limits frequency resolution below the 2nd harmonic but does not affect these conclusions.
- Full 12-harmonic coefficient tables are printed to stdout by the script.

The graphs show, per painting: the binarized image with traced edges, all phase-aligned copies overlaid on a pure sine, the deviation curve, and linear + dB amplitude spectra (green bars = phase-coherent harmonics, red dashed line = per-harmonic noise level).

## Results

### *Study for Arrest Series* (1965, gouache on graph paper)

![2026_CKS_24182_0636_000_bridget_riley_study_for_arrest_series114631.jpg](images/2026_CKS_24182_0636_000_bridget_riley_study_for_arrest_series114631.jpg)

- About this work: [https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6575115](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6575115)
- BRIDGET RILEY (B. 1931)
- *Study for Arrest Series*, 1965
- Signed, inscribed and dated 'Study. final painting completed '65 Bridget Riley.'
- gouache, graphite and ballpoint pen on graph paper
- 13 ¼ x 28 in. (33.7 x 71.1 cm.)


![analysis](out/arrest_analysis.png)

- Mean wavelength λ ≈ 330 px; fundamental amplitude c₁ ≈ 20 px ≈ 13 mm.
- **THD (harmonics 2–12): 3.4%** (per copy 1.3–5.4%).
- RMS deviation from the best-fit sine: 1.14 px ≈ 0.7 mm = 5.7% of the wave amplitude — but most of that is incoherent brush wobble (the 12-harmonic fit still leaves a 5.1% residual). The *systematic* distortion is the 3.4% THD, dominated by a 2nd harmonic at ψ ≈ −169°: the hand-painted wave is slightly asymmetric, one flank consistently differing from the other.

Phase-coherent harmonics (all others are noise-level, < 0.5%):

| n | cₙ/c₁ | dB rel. fundamental | ψₙ | phase coherence |
|---|-------|-----|------|------|
| 1 | 1.000 | 0 | 0° | 1.00 |
| 2 | 0.027 ± 0.013 | −31.5 | −169° | 0.77 |
| 3 | 0.016 ± 0.008 | −36.1 | −38° | 0.86 |

Painting a sine freehand to within a millimeter RMS is remarkable; the study
being on graph paper suggests Riley plotted the curve point by point.

- Mean wavelength λ ≈ 1291 px; fundamental amplitude c₁ ≈ 55 px.
- **THD (harmonics 2–12): 1.4%** (per copy 0.9–1.8%).
- RMS deviation from a pure sine: 1.07 px = 1.9% of the wave amplitude,
  barely above the tracing noise floor of 1.6%.
- The one unambiguous departure is a 3rd harmonic at 1% of the fundamental
  with phase coherence 0.98 across all 156 copies. At ψ₃ = +63° (with the
  2nd harmonic at −44°), it means the wave consistently *leans* very
  slightly rather than being flattened or peaked. A symmetric construction

## Bonus study: Wave similarity within each painting

**Key finding:** *Cataract 3* and *Gala* show evidence that a template was used to trace the waves. 

Within a given painting, are the waves *identical* to each other, or
does each wave deviate from a sine in its own unique way? To answer this, each
copy's systematic deviation-from-sine profile (its Fourier series minus the
fundamental, phase-aligned and normalized by the fundamental amplitude — a
dimensionless curve) is cross-correlated against every other copy's profile,
giving an N×N matrix of Pearson correlations per painting. 

Using this analysis, a pair of copies that were traced from the same template 
would deviate from the sine *in the same places, by the same amount*
and would correlate near +1. On the other hand, waves drawn with independent 
hand wobbles would correlate near 0. Because the Pearson `r` is scale- and offset-invariant, the distributions are directly comparable between paintings.

![similarity](out/similarity.png)

| painting | copies | pairs | median r | IQR | pairs with r > 0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *Study for Arrest Series* | 14 | 91 | 0.62 | 0.38 – 0.72 | 62% |
| *Cataract 3* | 169 | 14,196 | **0.85** | 0.77 – 0.89 | 96% |
| *Gala* | 156 | 12,090 | 0.82 | 0.75 – 0.88 | 99% |

- **The waves in *Cataract 3* and *Gala* were traced from a template.** In *Cataract 3* and *Gala* nearly every pair of
  copies shares the same deviation profile (median r ≈ 0.85). The waves are
  not merely all close to a sine — they are close to *the same* slightly
  imperfect sine, strong evidence that one master curve (template or
  transfer drawing) was reproduced across the whole canvas.
- **The hand-painted *Arrest* study has a broad distribution**: the distribution stretches from anticorrelated to near-identical (IQR 0.38–0.72). There is still a
  shared component — the copies are not independent doodles, consistent
  with tracing over a drawn guide — but each stroke adds its own deviation
  of comparable size. A few copies (visible as blue bands in the 14×14
  matrix) even deviate *oppositely* to the rest.
- The matrices order copies from the top of the painting to the bottom; the
  faint blocky texture in *Cataract 3* and *Gala* shows neighbouring
  stripes resembling each other slightly more than distant ones — drift of
  the template or of the photographic perspective across the canvas.
- **The diagonal ridges in the *Gala* matrix every ~41 rows mark the
  painting's phase recurrence.** Each successive stripe is phase-shifted by
  a steady ~18° (~9° per edge copy), so the wave returns to its starting
  phase every ~20 stripes ≈ 40 copies — exactly where the off-diagonal
  ridges sit (lags 38–42 and 79–82). Pairs of copies that are in phase
  correlate more strongly (mean r ≈ 0.87–0.92 vs ≈ 0.80 otherwise) because
  deviations fixed to the canvas or image frame — lens/perspective
  distortion, canvas stretch — are rotated by each copy's phase offset
  during alignment, and re-register only when two copies share the same
  phase. A small secondary uptick for pairs ~180° apart confirms this:
  anti-phase alignment re-registers the even harmonics of that
  canvas-fixed component. Simply put: diagonals are where the painting's 
  phase progression returns to its starting point, and their visibility 
  in the matrix is a fingerprint of canvas/image-frame distortions 
  (a few tenths of a percent of the amplitude) layered on top of the shared template.
- **Different paintings used different templates.** Cross-correlating the
  *mean* deviation profiles between paintings: *Cataract 3* vs *Gala* gives
  r = −0.08 (and +0.06 mirror-flipped) — their deviation-from-sine
  signatures are unrelated (*Gala*: h3 at +63°, h2 at −44°; *Cataract 3*:
  h2 at −138°, h3 at −37°). Nor could one physical object have served both:
  the wavelengths on canvas differ, ≈ 21 cm in the *Arrest* study, ≈ 56 cm
  in *Cataract 3* and ≈ 67 cm in *Gala* (amplitudes ≈ 1.3, 2.5 and 2.9 cm).
  One curious side-finding: *Arrest* vs *Cataract 3* correlate at r = +0.78,
  driven by nearly identical 3rd-harmonic phases (−38° vs −37°) and
  2nd-harmonic phases within ~30°. With only two or three harmonics
  dominating these profiles (and only 14 noisy copies in the study), this
  should not be over-read as a shared template — the scales differ by 2.6×
  regardless — but it is consistent with the two 1960s works sharing a
  *construction method* that leaves the same slight asymmetry, while *Gala*
  (1974) was built differently. In short: within each painting, one master
  curve reproduced many times; across paintings, three different curves.
